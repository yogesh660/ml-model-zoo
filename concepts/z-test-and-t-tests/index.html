01 The idea

When testing hypotheses about population means, the choice of test statistic hinges on whether population variance is known and whether sample sizes justify asymptotic normal approximations. The z-test applies when variance is known or sample sizes are large, whereas Student's and Welch's t-tests account for the extra uncertainty of estimating variance from small samples.

Imagine an engineering team modifies a database query engine to reduce latency below the historic benchmark of 120 ms. They benchmark 25 query runs and record a sample mean latency of 112 ms with a sample standard deviation $s = 15$ ms. Is this 8 ms reduction convincing evidence of improvement, or could it be routine variance in server load?

If we knew with absolute certainty from millions of historic runs that the true population standard deviation was $\sigma = 15$ ms, the standard error would be exactly $\sigma / \sqrt{25} = 3.0$ ms. The standard score would be $z = (112 - 120) / 3.0 = -2.67$, giving a two-sided p-value of approximately 0.0076 under the standard normal distribution.

However, in realistic settings we do not know the true $\sigma$; we must estimate it using $s$ from the very same 25 queries. When William Gosset analysed this problem at the Guinness Brewery in 1908 (publishing under the pen name "Student"), he proved that dividing by an estimated $s$ inflates sampling variability. The resulting $t$-distribution has heavier tails than the normal curve. For 24 degrees of freedom, $t = -2.67$ yields a p-value of approximately 0.0134—nearly double the normal p-value! Relying naively on a $z$-test when $s$ is estimated causes overconfidence and elevated false alarms.

In everyday terms

Using a normal z-test when you only have sample variance s is like driving at top speed on a foggy morning: it assumes clarity where there is hidden uncertainty, which the t-distribution's heavier tails properly account for.

02 Step by step

  1. Determine the test design: one-sample (mean vs standard), two-sample independent (group A vs group B), or paired (repeated measures on the same subjects).
  2. Verify assumptions: continuous data, random independent sampling, and approximate normality of sample means (guaranteed by CLT for $n \ge 30$).
  3. If population standard deviation $\sigma$ is known, compute the $z$-statistic; if estimated via $s$, compute the $t$-statistic.
  4. For two independent groups, adopt Welch's $t$-test by default to guard against unequal variances ($\sigma_1^2 \ne \sigma_2^2$).
  5. For paired observations, compute unit-level differences $D_i = X_{1,i} - X_{2,i}$ and perform a one-sample $t$-test on the differences.
  6. Evaluate the p-value against degrees of freedom $\nu$ and decide whether to reject the null hypothesis at threshold $\alpha$.

03 The maths

  1. One-Sample z-Statistic and Student's t-Statistic

    $$z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), \qquad t = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}} \sim t(n - 1)$$

    Here $\bar{X}$ is the sample mean, $\mu_0$ is the hypothesised benchmark, $\sigma$ is the known population standard deviation, and $s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum (X_i - \bar{X})^2}$ is the sample standard deviation. Gosset's $t$-statistic follows Student's $t$-distribution with $\nu = n - 1$ degrees of freedom, converging to $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ as $n \to \infty$.

  2. Welch's Two-Sample t-Test (Unequal Variances)

    $$t = \frac{\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2}{\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}, \qquad \nu \approx \frac{\left(\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}{\frac{(s_1^2 / n_1)^2}{n_1 - 1} + \frac{(s_2^2 / n_2)^2}{n_2 - 1}}$$

    Welch's $t$-test compares two independent group means without assuming equal population variances ($\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$). The Welch–Satterthwaite equation approximates the effective degrees of freedom $\nu$. Welch's test maintains robust Type I error control under heteroscedasticity where Student's pooled $t$-test breaks down.

  3. Paired t-Test for Dependent Samples

    $$D_i = X_{1,i} - X_{2,i}, \qquad t = \frac{\bar{D} - 0}{s_D / \sqrt{n}} \sim t(n - 1)$$

    For matched pairs or repeated measures (such as pre- and post-treatment values for the same users), between-subject variability is removed by taking differences $D_i$. Here $\bar{D}$ is the average difference and $s_D$ is the sample standard deviation of differences. Subtracting out subject baseline variation dramatically increases statistical power.

04 Key takeaways

  • Gosset's $t$-distribution accounts for the joint uncertainty of estimating both the population mean $\mu$ and population standard deviation $\sigma$.
  • Always prefer Welch's $t$-test over Student's pooled $t$-test for two independent samples; testing for equality of variances first creates pre-test bias.
  • Paired $t$-tests eliminate subject-to-subject variability by analysing intra-subject differences, providing far greater statistical power for matched cohorts.
  • Thanks to the central limit theorem, $t$-tests are remarkably robust to non-normal data once sample sizes exceed roughly 30 observations per group.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
import scipy.stats as stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n_users = 30

# Paired experiment: latency before and after database indexing
pre_latency = rng.normal(loc=72.0, scale=8.0, size=n_users)
# Each user improves by ~3.5 ms, plus individual variation
post_latency = pre_latency - rng.normal(loc=3.5, scale=4.0, size=n_users)

# 1. Paired t-test (correct: accounts for within-subject correlation)
t_pair, p_pair = stats.ttest_rel(pre_latency, post_latency)

# 2. Independent Welch t-test (incorrect: ignores pairing)
t_welch, p_welch = stats.ttest_ind(pre_latency, post_latency, equal_var=False)

mean_improvement = float(np.mean(pre_latency - post_latency))
print(f"Mean improvement: {mean_improvement:.2f} ms")
print(f"Paired t-test:    t={t_pair:.3f}, p-value={p_pair:.4e}")
print(f"Welch t-test:     t={t_welch:.3f}, p-value={p_welch:.4f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Applying an independent two-sample $t$-test to paired or before-and-after data, throwing away subject pairing and crippling statistical power.
  • Using Student's pooled $t$-test when group sample sizes and variances are unequal, which severely inflates the false positive rate.
  • Running a $t$-test on small samples ($n < 20$) with extreme skewness or severe outliers where the sample mean is not approximately normal.
  • Using a $z$-test when sample size is small and $\sigma$ is estimated from the sample, producing falsely narrow confidence bounds and deflated p-values.

06 Check yourself

Q1Why does Student's $t$-distribution have heavier tails than the standard normal distribution?

Why: The sample standard deviation $s$ fluctuates from sample to sample. Dividing the sample mean by a random denominator fattens the tails of the test statistic distribution.

Q2When comparing means between two independent groups of different sizes, why is Welch's $t$-test preferred over Student's pooled $t$-test?

Why: Student's pooled test assumes homoscedasticity ($\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$). When variances differ and group sizes are unequal, the pooled test has severely distorted Type I error rates. Welch's test does not require equal variances.

Q3In an experiment where 40 users complete tasks using Interface A and then Interface B, which test is appropriate to compare task completion times?

Why: Because the same 40 users were tested under both conditions, observations are paired. Taking within-user differences removes user-to-user variability and isolates the interface effect.

Q4As the degrees of freedom $\nu$ in Student's $t$-distribution approach infinity ($\nu \to \infty$), what does the distribution become?

Why: As $n \to \infty$, the sample standard deviation $s$ converges in probability to the true population standard deviation $\sigma$ by the law of large numbers, so $t(\nu)$ converges to $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.
