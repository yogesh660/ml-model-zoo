Correlation: Pearson, Spearman, Kendall

Measuring linear vs monotonic dependence: Pearson r for straight lines, Spearman rho for rank trends, and Kendall tau for concordant pairs.

01 The idea

Correlation standardises covariance into a dimensionless scale between -1 and +1, making associations comparable across features of completely different units. Choosing between Pearson, Spearman and Kendall determines whether your model looks for straight linear lines, rank monotonicity, or robust ordinal agreement in noisy data.

Suppose you track study hours and exam scores for 100 students. If every additional hour of studying reliably adds 3 percentage points to a score, the relationship is linear and Pearson's $r$ approaches $+1.0$. But now imagine an exponential learning curve: going from 1 to 2 hours doubles your score, but going from 20 to 21 hours barely registers a change. The relationship is strictly increasing (more study always helps), yet it is not a straight line. Pearson's $r$ drops because the line bends, but Spearman's rank correlation remains exactly $+1.0$.

This distinction between linear and monotonic relationships is crucial in feature selection and exploratory data analysis. Pearson assumes both variables are interval-scaled, roughly normal, and free of extreme outliers. A single outlier—such as a rogue data point with 100 study hours and a score of 0—can pull a strong Pearson correlation of $+0.85$ down to zero or even flip its sign.

Spearman and Kendall protect against this by discarding raw numerical distances and looking only at order. Spearman converts raw numbers to ranks ($1\text{st}, 2\text{nd}, 3\text{rd}\dots$) before applying Pearson's formula. Kendall takes an even cleaner combinatorial approach: it looks at every possible pair of students and counts whether the student with more study hours also got the higher exam score. This makes Kendall exceptionally robust on small sample sizes with heavy ties.

In everyday terms

Pearson checks if two runners maintain a constant speed ratio; Spearman checks if the first runner always finishes ahead of the second, regardless of the gap.

02 Step by step

  1. Inspect a scatter plot to diagnose whether the expected relationship is strictly linear, non-linear monotonic, or contaminated by outliers.
  2. Compute Pearson's $r$ to assess linear association when both features are continuous and approximately Gaussian: $r = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}$.
  3. If the trend is curved or heavily skewed, convert values to fractional ranks and compute Spearman's $\rho$.
  4. On small samples or data with frequent tied ranks, compute Kendall's $\tau$ by evaluating the proportion of concordant versus discordant pairs.
  5. Test statistical significance via hypothesis tests ($t$-test for Pearson, permutation or asymptotic $z$ for ranks) against the null hypothesis $H_0: \rho = 0$.

03 The maths

  1. Pearson Product-Moment Correlation

    $$r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}$$

    Pearson's $r \in [-1, 1]$ standardises covariance by dividing by the product of both standard deviations. It measures the strength and direction of a purely linear relationship. $r = \pm 1$ indicates that points lie exactly along a straight line with positive or negative slope.

  2. Spearman Rank Correlation

    $$\rho = 1 - \frac{6 \sum_{i=1}^n d_i^2}{n(n^2 - 1)}, \qquad d_i = \operatorname{rank}(x_i) - \operatorname{rank}(y_i)$$

    Spearman's $\rho$ is Pearson's $r$ computed on the ordinal ranks of $X$ and $Y$. The simplified difference formula applies when there are no tied ranks ($d_i$ is the rank discrepancy). It measures monotonic relationships: whether one variable increases when the other increases, regardless of linearity.

  3. Kendall's Tau

    $$\tau = \frac{C - D}{\frac{1}{2} n (n - 1)}$$

    Kendall's $\tau$ counts pairwise concordance across all $\binom{n}{2}$ distinct observation pairs $(x_i, y_i)$ and $(x_j, y_j)$. A pair is concordant ($C$) if $(x_i - x_j)(y_i - y_j) > 0$, and discordant ($D$) if negative. It offers superior small-sample efficiency and intuitive probabilistic interpretation: $P(\text{concordance}) - P(\text{discordance})$.

04 Key takeaways

  • Correlation measures association, never causation: a third lurking variable can induce strong spurious correlations.
  • Pearson is highly sensitive to outliers; a single distant rogue point can artificially inflate or destroy linear correlation.
  • Spearman and Kendall are invariant to any strictly monotonic transformation, such as taking logarithms or square roots.
  • Kendall's $\tau$ converges to a normal distribution much faster than Spearman's $\rho$ on small samples, making hypothesis testing more reliable.

05 In code

python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
x = np.linspace(1, 10, 50)
# Strictly monotonic non-linear relationship: y = exp(x / 3) + noise
y = np.exp(x / 3.0) + rng.normal(0, 0.5, size=50)

# Add an extreme outlier to demonstrate robustness
x_out = np.append(x, [10.0])
y_out = np.append(y, [0.0])

r_clean, _ = stats.pearsonr(x, y)
rho_clean, _ = stats.spearmanr(x, y)
r_dirty, _ = stats.pearsonr(x_out, y_out)
rho_dirty, _ = stats.spearmanr(x_out, y_out)
tau_clean, _ = stats.kendalltau(x, y)

print(f"Clean Data   -> Pearson r: {r_clean:.3f}, Spearman rho: {rho_clean:.3f}, Kendall tau: {tau_clean:.3f}")
print(f"With Outlier -> Pearson r: {r_dirty:.3f}, Spearman rho: {rho_dirty:.3f}")

Common pitfalls

  • Relying on Pearson's $r$ for non-linear associations (e.g. Anscombe's quartet or quadratic curves where $r \approx 0$ despite perfect dependence).
  • Confusing statistical significance ($p$-value) with practical significance; with a million rows, even $r = 0.01$ will have $p < 0.001$.
  • Computing Pearson correlation on ordinal survey responses (e.g. Likert scales) instead of rank-based Spearman or Kendall.
  • Assuming correlation implies zero multicollinearity; two features can have modest pairwise correlation yet cause severe multicollinearity in a multivariate regression.

06 Check yourself

Q1If $Y = \exp(X)$ for strictly positive $X$, what are the Pearson and Spearman correlations?

Why: The relationship is perfectly monotonic but non-linear. Spearman's rank correlation is strictly $1.0$, whereas Pearson's linear correlation is strictly less than $1.0$ due to curvature.

Q2How does a single extreme outlier typically affect Pearson's $r$ compared to Spearman's $\rho$?

Why: Pearson calculates squared Euclidean distances from the mean, so an outlier has immense leverage. Spearman ranks observations, so an outlier moves at most to the end of the ranking.

Q3What does a Kendall tau ($\tau$) of $+0.6$ mean probabilistically?

Why: Kendall's $\tau = P(\text{concordant}) - P(\text{discordant})$, so $\tau = 0.6$ means the concordance probability exceeds discordance by 0.6.

Q4Which correlation metric is most suitable for evaluating rank orderings on small samples with tied ranks?

Why: Kendall's $\tau$ has smaller gross error sensitivity, handles tied ranks cleanly via Kendall's tau-b, and has well-characterised small-sample variance.
